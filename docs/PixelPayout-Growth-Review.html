PixelPayout · growth review · revised after founder correction

Everything turns on numbers you have not measured yet

The app is well built — better built than almost anything in its category. Whether its economy works is not a question of design taste; it is a question of two figures nobody has measured, and every strong claim below is conditional on them.

Revised — what changed and why

The first version of this review was built on two premises I inferred from the code and your own documents, and both were wrong.

Premise 1: Pakistan-only. You have since retargeted globally, skewed low-tier. That moves blended rewarded eCPM from roughly $0.40 to roughly $1.40 — a 3.5× swing that runs through every figure in section 02. My headline finding ("one ad buys two-thirds of a star") was an artefact of the narrower market. At a global blend one ad buys about 2.3 stars, and the economy goes from structurally broken to high but survivable.

Premise 2: no distribution. You have a 100k-subscriber gaming channel plus ~50k across other platforms. Section 05 was written as though you were starting from zero, which was the single biggest error in the document.

What survives both corrections: the faucet is not pegged to measured revenue, you have no analytics, and nothing has shipped. Those were never premise-dependent.

02 The economy, and why it is upside down

Doing this before user acquisition, because acquisition strategy is downstream of whether a user is worth acquiring. Here, they are not — yet.

What a rewarded ad is worth

At a global blend of $1.40 eCPM, one rewarded impression pays about $0.0014 — Rs 0.39. At a placeholder Rs 0.17 per star, one ad funds roughly 2.3 stars. In a Pakistan-only mix it funded 0.66, which is why the first draft of this section read as a death sentence and this one does not.

An engaged user, per day

Twelve rewarded views a day, not the 26 the caps technically allow. Reward apps see a fraction of their theoretical ad ceiling, and planning against the ceiling is how these models flatter themselves.

This is the corrected picture, and it is a completely different business from the one in the first draft. 65% of gross revenue promised back to users is roughly 10 points too generous, not four times too generous. That is a tuning problem, and you already intend to tune the level table down. Do that and it lands inside normal.

Two caveats that decide whether the 65% is real. The traffic mix — at 90% tier-3 rather than 60%, blended eCPM falls to about $0.70 and the ratio goes back above 100%. And the 12 rewarded views — your argument that leaderboard competition and double-XP will push heavy users toward the 26-view ceiling is plausible and, if right, improves the ratio further. Both are measurable in week one and neither is knowable now.

How to actually set the star price — the formula, not a guess

Do not price stars against a currency. Price them against the first pack and a target grind length, and let the rupee figure fall out:

star price = pack wholesale cost ÷ (daily stars earned × target days)

Worked: a 60 UC pack costs you ~$0.90. You want the first one at 30 days. An engaged user earns ~26★/day. So the first pack is 780★ and one star is worth $0.00115. Undiscounted, at 60 days, the same pack is 1,560★ and a star is $0.00058 — which is almost exactly your Rs 0.17 placeholder. Your placeholder is already self-consistent with a 30-day discounted first redeem and a 60-day standard one. That is a better starting position than you probably realised.

The thing that is currently saving you: breakage

A star only costs money when it is redeemed. Everything above is a liability, not a loss. If a UC pack costs several thousand stars and a user quits at 400, you paid nothing at all.

This is real and it is how every points economy survives. It is also a trap, because breakage and trust are the same dial turned in opposite directions. High breakage means most people never got paid, which means your Play Store reviews say "fake app, I played 2 months and got nothing", which means acquisition dies. You cannot bank on breakage and build a trusted brand at the same time. Pick deliberately, and know which one you picked.

"Where it breaks" — you already solved this, so here is what I actually meant

My three scenarios assumed a catalogue priced at face value, where the cheapest thing on it was 325 UC at ~8,000★ — a payout ~285 days away that arithmetically never lands. Your design does not do that, and the confusion is mine for not saying which catalogue I was modelling. A discounted smallest-denomination first pack at ~30 days is precisely the right answer and it retires the concern.

The real cliff is the second redeem. First pack: 30 days, discounted, celebrated. Second pack: 60 days at full price, no discount, no novelty — twice the wait for the same reward. That is where a user who now trusts you decides you are not worth it, and they leave as a believer rather than a sceptic, which is worse because they tell people the app is real but not worth the grind. Watch retention in the fortnight after first payout more closely than any other window.

Lifetime value, and what it permits

Across 1,000 installs over 90 days — decent D1, brutal D30, roughly 3,500 active user-days weighted toward light users — ad revenue lands near $42 per 1,000 installs. LTV is about $0.04 per install, four times my Pakistan-only figure.

Revised: paid acquisition is marginal, not impossible

At $0.04 LTV, tier-3 CPI at $0.05–0.15 is close enough to argue about rather than absurd. It is still the wrong place for your money — you own a channel that delivers installs at zero marginal cost, and buying installs while an owned audience sits unused is the definition of a bad trade. But the earlier claim that paid UA is categorically unavailable was a Pakistan artefact. Treat a small paid test as a legitimate week-10 experiment, capped at a few hundred dollars, run only after the channel numbers are in and only to answer whether bought users behave like organic ones. They usually do not.

What has to change

  1. Build the offerwall, or accept the app cannot pay anyone. Offerwall RPM is 20–100× rewarded video. It is the only line item that can fund real payouts. It needs the S2S postback endpoint that does not exist. Everything else on your roadmap is decoration until this ships.
  2. Every star minted should trace to an impression or a conversion. Level rewards are already ad-gated — good instinct — but a 150-star level claim gated on one ad is 200× underwater. Either scale the reward toward what an ad pays, or make the big rewards offerwall-funded.
  3. Cut the fixed faucets. The daily-goal bonus at 10/day and the 2,450-star weekly leaderboard are outgoings that do not flex with revenue. The leaderboard at least shrinks per-user as you grow; the goal bonus does not.
  4. Stop treating the heavy player as the good user. They are your most expensive user by a wide margin, and under the current design they are the one you optimise for hardest.

03 The reframe: you are not building an earning app

This is the most valuable thing in the document. Everything about acquisition, hooks and trust changes depending on which sentence you believe.

Read as an earning app, PixelPayout is one of thousands, in the least trusted category on the Play Store, chasing users who want cash, in the market with the worst ad rates on earth. There is no version of that fight you win.

Read as what the code actually describes — a catalogue containing nothing but UC and Diamonds, a level gate, a player-ID link, a discounted first redeem — it is something much narrower:

What you have actually built

A way for a fifteen-year-old with no debit card to get UC without asking his parents for money.

That user is not "someone who wants to earn". He is a PUBG or Free Fire player who watches his squad buy skins, cannot ask for Rs 1,400, and has no payment instrument even if he could — Easypaisa and JazzCash want a CNIC and eighteen years. Midasbuy is official, instant and trusted, and completely unreachable to him. That is a real, unserved, emotionally loaded gap, and it is the only defensible position on your board.

Everything follows from the switch:

Two things to check before you commit to this

Trademark and Play policy. You cannot put "PUBG" or "Free Fire" in your store title, icon or ad creative. Approach the position obliquely — "game credit, no card needed" — and never imply endorsement.

Supply legitimacy. Where do you source the UC? If it comes from grey-market resellers, a topped-up account can be banned by Krafton or Garena. One banned account posted in a clan group ends the business overnight. Get this answered before you scale — a distributor relationship or official reseller route is worth more than any feature on your roadmap.

05 How I would actually get users

Constrained by section 02: no paid installs, ever, at current LTV. Every channel below is organic, barter, or founder labour.

First 10

Your own squad

Cost Rs 0 · Difficulty trivial · Quality highest you will ever have · Scalability none

People you can phone. The job is not growth, it is watching ten humans hit the account wall, the cold start and the XP-not-stars moment while you sit next to them. Ship nothing else until you have watched all ten.

First 100

Hand-recruited from gaming Facebook groups

Cost ~Rs 5,000 in real payouts · Difficulty low · Conversion high · Quality high · Risk: group admins delete promo posts

Pakistani PUBG and Free Fire Facebook groups run 50k–500k members. Do not post an ad. Post as a person: "I built an app that gets you UC without a card. I'll pay the first 20 people who actually test it and tell me what's broken." Then pay all twenty, publicly, in the thread, with screenshots.

The thread itself is the asset. It is dated, public, has real names in it, and it is the thing you link to for the next two years. Do the same in three or four Discord servers and any clan WhatsApp group you can get into — Pakistan runs on WhatsApp, and a forward inside a clan group outperforms every ad network on earth.

Correction — you are not starting from zero

A 100k-subscriber Delta Force channel, ~50k across other platforms, shorts reliably clearing 10k views. This is the single most valuable asset in the business, worth more than the codebase, and the first draft of this section did not know it existed.

Rough arithmetic: 36 shorts over a quarter at 10k views and a 0.5–2% install rate is 2,000–7,000 installs at zero cash cost, before a single long-form video or community post. That covers the entire "first 1,000" and most of "first 10,000" below, so read those stages as descriptions of quality control rather than of volume.

Two things to be careful about. Delta Force is a tactical shooter audience; PUBG Mobile UC buyers overlap but are not the same people — test one short before assuming the audience converts. And the channel is a one-shot asset: if the app takes payouts and does not deliver, you do not lose an acquisition channel, you lose the channel. That risk belongs in section 14 above almost everything else in it.

First 1,000

Your own channel, then micro-creators paid in UC

Cost Rs 20–60k or equivalent in UC · Difficulty medium · Conversion medium-high · Quality medium · Risk: one bad creator frames you as a scam

Not the 500k-subscriber channels — they cost real money and their audience is broad. Target Pakistani gaming channels and TikTok accounts at 5k–50k followers, where the audience is a clan rather than a demographic. Twenty of them at 50 installs each is your thousand.

Pay them in UC and a revenue share, not cash. They want UC more than they want Rs 3,000, it costs you your discounted rate rather than face value, and a creator who is earning through their own code keeps posting without being asked. Give every one a vanity code and show them their own dashboard.

Run your own TikTok in parallel from day one. It costs nothing but your time and it is the only channel here that can produce an outlier.

First 10,000

Referral becomes the product

Cost ~Rs 25 per install in stars · Difficulty high · Quality high · Scalability good · Risk: farming

Do the arithmetic on what you already have: your referrer bonus is 100 stars — Rs 17 — and the referee gets 50. Total acquisition cost around Rs 25, in currency you mint, versus Rs 28–56 in cash for a low-quality bought install. Your referral program already beats paid UA and you are underpaying it.

Three changes: raise the referrer reward to 300–500 stars; move the unlock from 100 XP to the referee's first successful redemption, so you only pay for users who reached the moment that matters; and add Play Integrity before you do either, because at 500 stars a fake account becomes worth manufacturing.

The mechanic that actually spreads, though, is not the bonus. It is squad redemption: let four players pool stars into one pack for one member. It converts a solitary grind into a group project, it makes the inviter socially accountable, and no reward app in this market does it. That is section 09 in full.

First 100,000

Store search, plus the payout feed as public record

Cost near zero · Difficulty high · Risk: policy rejection, reputation collapse

At this scale organic Play search is the engine, and it is won by review score and payout proof, not by ASO tricks. Your masked payout feed is already the right instinct — take it further and publish it outside the app, as a public page with a running total. "Rs 340,000 paid to 4,100 players" on a live page is the only marketing that works in a category defined by lying.

Be honest that this stage is conditional. It only arrives if the offerwall funds real payouts and the reviews hold above 4.2. Below that, growth stops regardless of channel.

Channels I would refuse

"Online earning" YouTube channels. The obvious channel in Pakistan, and a trap. Their audience installs every app mentioned, farms it, and churns in 72 hours. You get an install spike, a collapsing D7, an AdMob invalid-traffic flag, and reviewers who have already reviewed forty apps exactly like yours.

Any CPI network. See section 02. At $0.01 LTV this is buying banknotes for a dollar each.

Written for a teenage PUBG player scrolling TikTok, not for an investor. The strongest ones counter-position against the category's own reputation rather than competing with it.

  2. Rs 1,400, or twenty minutes a day for three weeks. Your call.Honest about the price in time. Makes the reader do the maths themselves, which is far more persuasive than doing it for them.
  3. No card. No Easypaisa. No asking anyone.Names the three walls in nine words. Every under-18 in Pakistan has hit all three.

  5. The UC goes into your account. Not into a wallet you can't withdraw from.Attacks the specific mechanism every fake app uses to never pay: the unreachable minimum withdrawal.
  6. Your parents will never know where the UC came from.The actual emotional job. Use with judgement — it's the truest line here and the one most likely to draw complaints.

  • Screenshot 1 is a payout, not a feature. UC landing in a game account, with the date on it. Most apps waste this slot on a logo.

What is actually defensible

Being blunt: none of your features are a moat. The level curve, streak, goals, leaderboard and payout feed are two weeks of work for a competent team. The server-side answer keys and session-bound game rewards are genuinely good engineering, and they are invisible to users and copyable by anyone.

Three things could become moats, none of them code:

  1. A public, unbroken payout record. Slow to build, impossible to fake, and it compounds. In a category where trust is the binding constraint, this is the whole game. Start the public ledger with payout number one.
  2. Supply. A real distributor relationship for UC and easyload at 2–4% off face is margin nobody can copy without doing the same paperwork — and it is the difference between paying Rs 1,390 and Rs 1,340 on every redemption, forever.
  3. The panel. Your Panel Revenue Playbook is right about this: a retained, verified panel of Pakistani gamers is the one asset that is hard to assemble. It is also, correctly, parked until ~2,000 DAU.

Note what all three share: they are earned over time, not built in a sprint. Which means your competitive position at launch is zero, and your job for ninety days is to start the clock on all three rather than to add features.

10 Referral, done properly

Given that paid acquisition is unavailable, this is not a feature. It is your marketing department.

The economics you already have

Referrer 100 stars + referee 50 stars = Rs 25 per acquired user, in currency you mint and which only costs you on redemption. Compare a bought install at Rs 28–56 in cash with worse retention. You are running the cheaper channel and funding it like an afterthought.

At 400 stars to the referrer you would pay Rs 76 per install — still competitive, because the user arrives pre-trusted by a friend and retains several times better. The headroom here is enormous and completely unused.

The redesign

  1. Inviter gets 400 stars, unlocked on the referee's first successful redemption. Not 100 XP. You should pay for outcomes you can see, and a referee who redeemed is a referee who is real, engaged and now has their own screenshot.
  2. Referee gets a shortcut, not stars. An invited user's first-redeem discount is deeper, or their level-10 gate drops to level 6. It costs less than stars, it is worth more to them, and it makes accepting an invite meaningfully better than installing cold.
  3. Squad redemption. Four accounts pool stars into one pack for one member, taking turns. This is the mechanic that would actually spread: it turns a solitary grind into a group obligation, it gives the inviter a reason to chase their friends daily, and it maps exactly onto how PUBG is played. Nobody in this market has it.
  4. Milestone ladder, not a flat rate. 3 referrals unlocks a bracketed leaderboard tier; 10 unlocks a permanent daily-cap raise. Caps cost XP, not stars — you already know this from the VIP analysis.

Fraud, honestly

At 400 stars a fake account becomes worth manufacturing, and Settings.Secure.ANDROID_ID resets on a factory reset. Your own register already flags this. Play Integrity has to ship before the reward rises, not after. Then: unlock on first redemption (a farmed account must complete a real redemption to pay out, which is expensive to fake), one payout per device attestation, and a manual review queue above a referral count you set. The redemption approval step is already manual — that is your backstop, and it is a genuinely good one.

Does it pay for itself

Only if the referred user's lifetime revenue exceeds Rs 76. At $0.01 LTV — Rs 2.80 — it does not. Nothing does. This is worth saying plainly: at current revenue per user, no acquisition channel is profitable, including this one. The referral is the cheapest way to lose money per user, which is the correct thing to optimise for only while you are buying information rather than growth. It becomes genuinely profitable the moment the offerwall works and not one day before.

11 Trust

Your user has been lied to by this category before. Assume it and design for the assumption.

You have already built more of this than most: masked payout feed, pending-payout row, order history, a declined-redemption dialog that explains itself, a paid-payout takeover. The instinct is right. What is missing is that the trust evidence is buried behind the loop instead of in front of it.

What Home should show, in order

  1. The payout feed at the top, not the bottom. Three live lines, above the star card. It is the first thing a suspicious new user should see, and right now it sits below the fold under goals and streak.
  2. A dated countdown to a specific named pack. "100 Diamonds — 11 days at your pace." Concrete, falsifiable, and the opposite of every fake app's infinite progress bar.
  3. A lifetime paid counter. "Rs 340,000 paid to 4,100 players." Update it from the same write that fills the payout feed.
  4. The exact earning rate, stated. "A quiz pays 10 XP. Quizzes and games pay no stars — stars come from levels, goals and streaks." You already say this in the level-rewards note; move it somewhere it will be read.
  5. A visible fulfilment window. "Payouts are checked by hand and sent within 24 hours." Manual approval sounds like a weakness; framed as a human checking every payout, it is a feature.

Elsewhere

  • A face. One founder photo and a real name in the app and on the store listing. No fake app has ever done this, because they can't.
  • A support channel that answers. A WhatsApp number that replies within a day beats any in-app ticket system in this market.
  • Publish the odds and the rates before anyone asks. Free to do, and it removes the question permanently.
  • Never promise cash. Architecturally you don't, and marketing that limitation as a deliberate anti-scam stance is the strongest trust position available to you.

13 Where the flywheel breaks

The intended loop is: discover → install → earn → redeem → tell a friend → repeat. Yours breaks in three places, and they compound.

  1. Earn → redeem. If first payout is weeks away, the loop never completes even once. Nothing downstream can run. Fix: a real payout in week one.
  2. Redeem → tell a friend. A payout that produces no artefact produces no referral. UC landing in a game account is the artefact — but the app currently does nothing to help capture it. Fix: the paid-payout takeover should generate a shareable image, and the deferred five-star share bonus should ship with it.
  3. More activity → more revenue → better rewards. This link is severed. More activity currently means more cost, because the marginal activity mints stars and the marginal ad pays a fraction of one. Until the offerwall exists, your flywheel spins backwards under load: growth makes the loss bigger.

The third is the one that matters. It is not a UX problem or a funnel problem; it is arithmetic, and it is why the offerwall sits at the top of the priority list in section 17 despite being the least interesting thing to build.

17 If it were my money

Seven things, in order. Not a backlog — a sequence, where each one is worthless before the one above it.

  1. Answer the catalogue question. What does the cheapest pack cost in stars, and how many days is that at a real play rate? If the answer is over three weeks, nothing else on this list matters. One hour of work.
  2. Real ad IDs, one offerwall, the S2S endpoint. The only revenue that can fund payouts. Two weeks. Dull, and it is the whole business.
  3. Instrument the funnel. Analytics before users, not after. You are about to spend ninety days generating information you currently cannot read. Three days.
  4. Move the signup wall and put a payout inside week one. The two largest funnel leaks, both cheap to fix. One week.
  5. Get 100 real users out of gaming groups and pay 20 of them in public. Not "launch" — recruit. Rs 5,000 and two weeks of your own posting.
  6. Rebuild referral as the growth engine, behind Play Integrity. It is already cheaper than any bought install and you are funding it like an afterthought. Two weeks.
  7. Then decide, on data, whether Pakistan is the market. The same code with a tier-1 audience faces 20–50× the eCPM. It also faces real competitors and $2+ CPIs. That is a genuine fork, and you should reach it holding numbers rather than opinions.

What I would not do: spins, chests, VIP, tournaments, the panel playbook, more games, more quiz categories, or another design document. Every one of them is work you are capable of doing well, aimed at a company that does not exist yet.

What I would actually say to you

The engineering here is well above what this category produces. The economy reasoning in your own register is better than most funded startups manage, and the comment in rewardConfig.ts explaining why the bonus-attempt cap is the security is the kind of thinking that takes people years. What has not changed: you have not put it in front of anybody. There is no versionCode 2. Every artefact in the repo is depth — a decision register, a revenue playbook for 10,000 DAU, a deferred-work file with cost-of-delay analysis — and none of it is contact with a user. That was the real finding in the first draft and it survives every correction, because it was never about the numbers.

The new top risk is one neither of us listed before. You are about to point a 150,000-person audience you spent years building at an app that has never processed a single real payout. That audience is not a channel you can re-buy. Every recommendation in this document about small first payouts, visible receipts, honest timelines and manual review exists to protect it — not to protect the economy, which will survive a bad quarter, but to protect the one asset that will not.

Three months from now the useful thing to have is not a better app. It is a hundred people who used it, twenty who got paid, and a number telling you whether any of them told a friend.
